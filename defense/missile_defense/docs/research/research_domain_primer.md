## Details

### PART 1 — DOMAIN KNOWLEDGE MAP (what to learn, in priority order)

**Tier 1 — Learn first (the mission and its physics)**

**A. The missile-warning kill chain and public timelines.**
The kill chain runs: boost-phase detection (hot plume) → event typing/characterization → launch-point and predicted-impact-point estimation → handover to missile defense fire control. Legacy space assets (DSP, SBIRS) cue ground and maritime radars, which build fire-control tracks; the Joint Tactical Ground Station (JTAGS) disseminates in-theater warning over Link 16 and the Integrated Broadcast Service. NORAD/USNORTHCOM own the strategic warning mission; MDA's C2BMC ties sensors to shooters.
Public timeline anchors you can cite:
- Boost phase for a solid-propellant ICBM is roughly 180 seconds; theater-range ballistic missile boost is typically 60–150 seconds (Wilkening, *Science & Global Security*, 2004; Wikipedia "Missile defense").
- Terminal phase generally lasts less than 60 seconds (US patent 7,551,121 background).
- Legacy DSP detection/processing chain is described in one open source (INSS, March 2025) as averaging about 30 seconds from ignition to alert, with interceptor prep/launch ~100 seconds after alert — treat these as illustrative open-literature figures, not requirements.
- A National Academies boost-phase study frames "the time needed to detect and characterize a threatening launch—probably on the order of 1 min."
Best sources: CRS "Hypersonic Missile Defense: Issues for Congress" (IF11623); the Arms Control Association boost-phase pieces; the National Academies boost-phase report (nap.edu/read/13189); CSIS "Getting on Track."

**B. Threat classes and signatures.** Ballistic missiles (bright boost plume, predictable Keplerian midcourse, decoy-vulnerable), hypersonic glide vehicles (dim, aerodynamically maneuvering in the glide phase, lower and unpredictable), and Fractional Orbital Bombardment Systems (partial-orbit approach, e.g., over the South Pole to evade north-facing radars). The core sensing shift: HGVs are dimmer than ICBM plumes and maneuver, so they are hard for GEO OPIR to hold custody on and defeat classical constant-velocity/constant-acceleration filters — motivating LEO/MEO proximity, IMM filters, and ML. Best sources: CSIS "Getting on Track"; CRS IF11623; the Science & Global Security HGV modeling paper (Tracy & Wright).

**C. OPIR phenomenology.** SWIR/MWIR bands (the classic MWIR 3–5 µm atmospheric window is used because it is the plume-signature band while suppressing some surface clutter); scanning vs. staring sensors (scanning WFOV for wide-area detection, staring/MFOV for precise tracks); background clutter (clouds, terrain, Earth-limb, sun glint); detection thresholds; false-alarm sources (aircraft, fires, specular reflections). The key SDA design tension is public: WFOV cameras "sweep large swathes of the skies... cover more potential launches; whereas medium field-of-view cameras can cover less volume of space but can provide higher resolution images and better see dim hypersonic missiles" (Breaking Defense). Best sources: CSIS "Getting on Track" (uses NASA IR scene data and a CSIS hypersonic vehicle signature figure); Aerospace Corporation OPIR primers; MSS (Military Sensing Symposia) if accessible.

**Tier 2 — Learn second (architecture and tracking math)**

**D. Architecture concepts.** Revisit rate, coverage, stereo/multi-look tracking (two lines-of-sight give a 3D fix), sensor tasking, fire-control-quality tracks and custody, latency budgets, ground vs. on-orbit processing, optical crosslinks (OCTs), and Link 16 / tactical data links carried via the SDA Transport Layer. The "ground migrates with space via edge fusion" vision is the topic's own phrasing — anchor your CONOPS to it. Best sources: CSIS "Getting on Track"; SDA fact sheets (sda.mil/on-orbit); eoPortal SDA page.

**E. Multi-target tracking and data fusion.** IMM (Interacting Multiple Model, for maneuvering targets), JPDA (Joint Probabilistic Data Association), MHT (Multiple Hypothesis Tracking), track-to-track fusion and the covariance-consistency/double-counting problem, bearing-only/angles-only tracking from space and its observability limits (a single LOS is unobservable in range; stereo or maneuver is required), and Cramér–Rao lower bounds for accuracy. Best sources: the Stone Soup framework and its papers (isif.org, arXiv 2506.07889, 2503.01293); Bar-Shalom's *Estimation with Applications to Tracking and Navigation* (standard textbook); Blackman & Popoli.

**Tier 3 — Learn third (programs and documentation conventions)**

**F. Current program landscape & ground segment.**
- **SBIRS / DSP:** legacy GEO + HEO IR early warning (Lockheed Martin built; USSF owns space and ground segments and the data).
- **Next-Gen OPIR (NGG GEO / NGP Polar):** SBIRS replacement. GAO-21-105249 (Sept 22, 2021) states "The U.S. Space Force plans to spend around $14.4 billion over the next 5 years to develop the Next Gen OPIR system." Lockheed Martin builds the GEO satellites; RTX (Raytheon) builds the IR payloads. Per GAO's 2025 annual assessment (via Breaking Defense, June 2025), the first NGG GEO satellite — though delivered as planned in September 2025 — "will be launched no earlier than March 2026" due to the launch backlog.
- **SDA Tracking Layer (PWSA):** proliferated LEO WFOV OPIR at ~1,000 km, 80–89.5° inclination. Tranche 0 = 8 tracking SVs (L3Harris/SpaceX). Per the SDA press release (July 18, 2022), Tranche 1 = 28 tracking satellites, with L3Harris ($700M) and Northrop Grumman ($617M) each building 14 — "a total of 28 satellites in four planes"; Director Derek Tournear said they launch in batches of seven to polar orbits "about 600 miles above Earth," first launch projected April 2025. Tranche 2 = 54 satellites (~$2.5B to L3Harris/Lockheed Martin/Sierra Space, January 2024). The Transport Layer provides the low-latency mesh and Link 16 injection.
- **MEO RMW/MT ("Epoch"):** Resilient Missile Warning/Missile Tracking in MEO. Epoch 1 = 12 SVs (Millennium Space Systems, a Boeing subsidiary; launching from late 2026). Per SSC (announced June 2025), BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems won a "$1.2 billion" Epoch 2 other-transaction agreement for 10 satellites with first delivery in FY2029, using the Trek bus with WFOV MWIR sensors; BAE completed CDR (announced Sept 21, 2026). Planned "baseline" MEO constellation ~30 satellites.
- **HBTSS (MDA):** MFOV "fire-control-quality" sensor; two prototypes launched Feb 14, 2024; WFOV cues HBTSS MFOV. In Flight Test Other-40 (FTX-40), "Stellar Banshee," on March 24, 2025 off Kauai, USS Pinckney (DDG 91) used Aegis Sea Based Terminal Increment 3 to "detect, track and perform a simulated engagement" (simulated SM-6) of an air-launched MRBM with an HTV-1 front end; the event "also provided a data collection opportunity for the Hypersonic and Ballistic Tracking Space Sensor (HBTSS) demonstration satellite" (MDA/DVIDS; MDA Director Lt. Gen. Heath Collins quoted).
- **FORGE:** the government-owned open ground framework. RTX built the Mission Data Processing Application Framework (MDPAF, $197M, 2020–2025), declared operational at the OPIR Battlespace Awareness Center (OBAC) in May 2024. SSC then awarded SciTec the FORGE Enterprise OPIR Solution (EOS) prototype on Apr. 24, 2025 ("$259 million," via a competitive SpEC OTA); SSC FORGE materiel leader Lt. Col. Daniel Groller said "EOS expands the aperture for industry innovation and will take the FORGE framework to the next level." This is your Phase III integration target.
- **Golden Dome:** the 2025 executive-order multi-layer homeland missile defense effort (space-based interceptors + sensor/custody layers). The White House cited a "$175 billion" estimate (May 20, 2025); CBO (May 12, 2026) estimated a notional architecture at "$1.2 trillion over the next two decades," with the space-based interceptor layer "about 70 percent of acquisition costs and 60 percent of total costs" — roughly 7,800 near-polar-orbit interceptors (~$720B) to engage 10 near-simultaneous ICBMs. The sensor/HBTSS/custody layers are why this topic is strategically funded.
- **Combined Program Office:** in 2022 the Space Force established a Combined Program Office to coordinate SDA PWSA, MDA HBTSS, and SSC's MEO satellites.
Best sources: CRS IF11623; GAO-21-105249 and GAO annual assessments; SDA.mil; SSC.spaceforce.mil press releases; eoPortal.

**G. DoDAF views and CONOPS conventions.** For a 30-page Space Force feasibility volume, produce a small, correct subset of DoDAF 2.02 views rather than the full set: AV-1 (overview/summary), OV-1 (high-level operational concept graphic — the one-picture mission cartoon for decision-makers), OV-2 (operational resource flow), OV-5b (activity model — your kill-chain decomposition), OV-6c (event-trace — the warn-to-decision timeline), SV-1 (systems interface), and SvcV/SV-7 measures matrices tying to accuracy/latency/resilience/coverage. Authoritative reference: the DoD CIO DoDAF 2.02 site (dodcio.defense.gov/DoDAF) and AcqNotes. Write the OV-1 first; it is the artifact the evaluators read first.

1. **What public latency/timeline numbers can I anchor to?** (Highest priority — your whole value proposition is "compressing timelines.") 3. **What is unclassified vs. classified?** Unclassified: public TLEs, published EO/IR physics, open HGV equations of motion, notional constellation geometry, your algorithms and their performance on synthetic data. State this boundary explicitly in the proposal — evaluators reward candor here.
4. **What does "fire-control quality" mean quantitatively?** Public sources describe it qualitatively (WFOV cues, MFOV/HBTSS provides "weapons-control-quality data"). The numeric track-accuracy thresholds are classified; acknowledge and propose to calibrate against them inside the TAP Lab in Phase II.
5. **Where does ML actually beat classical filters, and can it be explainable enough for a nuclear-adjacent warning mission?** The topic demands "sufficient confidence and explainability." This is a required section, not optional.

### PART 3 — PUBLIC DATA & TOOLS FOR A CREDIBLE UNCLASSIFIED SIMULATION

- **Orbital elements:** CelesTrak (celestrak.org) and Space-Track.org (free login, run by the 18th Space Defense Squadron) provide TLEs/OMMs for public catalog objects, propagatable with SGP4. SDA Tranche 0 tracking satellites are in the public catalog (e.g., T0TR objects, COSPAR 2023-050x); most tracking/MEO warning satellites have public orbital slots even where payloads are opaque. Realistic approach: propagate the actual public LEO tracking objects where cataloged, and model MEO/GEO/planned-tranche geometry from published counts and orbits.
- **Ballistic/HGV trajectory models:** Tracy & Wright, "Modeling the Performance of Hypersonic Boost-Glide Missiles" (*Science & Global Security*, 2020) gives the full 3-DOF equations of motion (lift, drag, gravity, centrifugal), L/D and roll-angle maneuver modeling, and RK integration — directly reusable. Wilkening (*S&GS* 2004) gives ballistic boost profiles. The Berkeley BRSL "Simulating Hypersonic Glide Vehicles" (Cameron Tracy, 2026) is an open-source modeling walk-through. Multiple open papers give winged-cone/HTV-2 parameter sets.
- **IR signature modeling:** CSIS "Getting on Track" documents its use of NASA IR scene data and a CSIS-derived hypersonic vehicle signature; use published plume/hardbody radiance figures and standard atmosphere (US Standard Atmosphere 1976) with MWIR band models. Be explicit that signatures are notional.
- **Tracking/astrodynamics libraries (open source):** Stone Soup (Python; IMM, JPDA, MHT, UKF, GOSPA/OSPA and PCRLB metrics, sensor management, MIT-licensed, backed by four allied defense labs — DRDC, Dstl, DSTG, NRL) is your tracking baseline. Orekit (Java, high-fidelity propagation/estimation), Skyfield/SGP4 (Python, TLE propagation), Basilisk (CU Boulder modular astrodynamics, message-based, Python/C++), GMAT (NASA), and poliastro's maintained successors are all appropriate. A Rust/Go core wrapping SGP4 plus Python Stone Soup for the tracker is a defensible, reproducible stack.
- **Public datasets:** direct space-based IR detection datasets are essentially unavailable publicly. Proxies: OpenSky Network ADS-B (used in Stone Soup MTT papers as a multi-target benchmark), GOES/VIIRS/DMSP imagery (the same unclassified sources the TAP Lab lists on its data page, and the basis for CU Boulder's GOES launch-detection work). Realistic assessment: you cannot validate against real OPIR track quality without the TAP Lab's classified enclave, so your Phase I claim must be feasibility-on-synthetic-data with a stated Phase II calibration plan.

### PART 6 — ITERATIVE EXPERIMENT LADDER

Each rung states a hypothesis, what it teaches, the public inputs needed, and the output artifact tied to a Phase II metric.

- **Rung 1 — Coverage/revisit over a launch region.** *Hypothesis:* public LEO/MEO/GEO geometry yields quantifiably different revisit and multi-look coverage over a chosen launch box. *Teaches:* the coverage/revisit fundamentals and the LEO proximity advantage. *Inputs:* CelesTrak TLEs (SDA T0 tracking + notional tranche geometry), SGP4/Skyfield. *Output:* coverage/revisit heatmap and n-fold-look statistics over the region → **coverage**.
- **Rung 2 — Add a synthetic ballistic + HGV trajectory and a detection model.** *Hypothesis:* a dim, maneuvering HGV is detectable/holdable for a smaller fraction of its flight than a bright ballistic boost. *Teaches:* signature/geometry-driven detectability, the HGV sensing gap. *Inputs:* Tracy & Wright HGV EOM, Wilkening ballistic profile, MWIR radiance + US Std Atmosphere, notional NEI threshold. *Output:* detectability-vs-time and stereo-availability curves → **coverage/accuracy**.
- **Rung 3 — Baseline tracking with Stone Soup.** *Hypothesis:* an IMM/JPDA baseline holds the ballistic track well but degrades on the maneuvering HGV. *Teaches:* where classical filters break, giving the honest baseline your ML must beat. *Inputs:* Stone Soup IMM/JPDA, simulated multi-sensor LOS detections + clutter. *Output:* OSPA/GOSPA error and track-continuity vs. maneuver intensity → **accuracy**.
- **Rung 4 — Compare fusion placement (ground vs. on-orbit vs. hybrid).** *Hypothesis:* moving association/fusion on-orbit reduces warn-to-decision time without proportional accuracy loss for some threat classes. *Teaches:* the core "edge fusion" trade — the heart of your proposal. *Inputs:* rungs 1–3 plus a link/latency model (crosslink vs. downlink-then-process). *Output:* warn-to-decision time vs. track accuracy Pareto frontier across placements → **latency + accuracy** (the headline chart).
- **Rung 5 — Add link degradation and satellite loss.** *Hypothesis:* proliferated + on-orbit fusion degrades more gracefully under node/link loss than centralized ground fusion. *Teaches:* resilience quantification, aligning with "contested environments." *Inputs:* rung-4 model with stochastic sat/link dropout. *Output:* accuracy/latency vs. % nodes lost degradation curves → **resilience**.
- **Rung 6 — Where ML beats classical filters (and stays explainable).** *Hypothesis:* a learned association or model-selection component (e.g., learned IMM model-set switching, or a learned data-association prior) reduces track loss on maneuvering HGVs versus fixed IMM/JPDA, with quantifiable added latency and an explainability wrapper. *Teaches:* the precise, defensible ML value claim plus the explainability story the topic demands. *Inputs:* rungs 3–5 plus a labeled synthetic maneuver dataset; RL/learned components (cf. Stone Soup RL sensor-management paper). *Output:* track-loss reduction and false-track rate, classical vs. ML, with confidence/explainability metrics → **accuracy + latency**.

### PART 7 — RED-TEAM CHECKLIST (how a MITRE/Aerospace evaluator attacks a software newcomer, and how to pre-empt)

1. **"Your fidelity is toy-grade."** Pre-empt: state every assumption with a public citation; use CSIS "Getting on Track" notional parameters as your defensible baseline; clearly label notional vs. sourced values; show a sensitivity analysis so conclusions don't hinge on any single guessed number.
2. **"You didn't validate against anything real."** Pre-empt: concede that real OPIR validation requires the classified enclave; validate your tracker against Stone Soup's published benchmarks and OpenSky ADS-B; propose Phase II calibration inside the TAP Lab as an explicit closure measure for this exact risk.
3. **"Your assumptions about sensitivity/latency are invented."** Pre-empt: never state an absolute classified-adjacent number as fact; report *relative* improvements; parameterize NEI/latency and show results across a range.
4. **"STK/SMARTSet and our FFRDC tools already do this."** Pre-empt: position your contribution as open, reproducible, fast-to-iterate, and CI-testable — a trade-space *exploration* engine (the topic's own words) that complements, not replaces, STK; be explicit that you're not claiming higher fidelity than STK, only faster and more transparent trade iteration.
5. **"Where does ML actually help, and can you trust it in a nuclear-adjacent mission?"** Pre-empt: make the ML claim narrow and measured (rung 6), quantify the classical baseline it beats, and dedicate a section to explainability/confidence — the topic explicitly requires "sufficient confidence and explainability."
6. **"You have no clearances, no space-sensing background, no defense past — how do you integrate into the TAP Lab?"** Pre-empt: turn the software-heavy, open-source pedigree into the asset (MOSA/edge software is exactly the TAP Lab's need); present a concrete clearance/sponsorship/onboarding plan; name the unclassified data you'll use first; recruit one credentialed advisor for domain review within the one-third subcontract cap.
7. **"Your CONOPS is written from the algorithm outward."** Pre-empt: follow the topic's ordering literally — system level first, then your technology's functions; lead with an OV-1 of the missile-warning enterprise and place your component inside it.
8. **"Three months, five CTEs — is this scoped realistically?"** Pre-empt: publish a tight CTE list (e.g., edge-fusion placement model; learned association/model-selection; covariance-consistent track-to-track fusion; explainability wrapper) and a research plan that maps each experiment rung to a CTE and a Phase II metric.

## Caveats

- **Two different TAP Labs are frequently conflated in public reporting.** The OPIR TAP Lab (Boulder, missile warning, boulderlab.org) is your target; the SDA TAP Lab (Colorado Springs, space domain awareness, "Project Apollo") is a sister organization. The three-month "apps to close gaps" cohort model and the Furlich GOES launch-detection work are most cleanly documented at the SDA lab; treat the cohort mechanics as the closest available proxy for OPIR-lab onboarding, not a guaranteed identical process. Boulderlab.org also shows an internal inconsistency between "monthly facility tours" (homepage) and "quarterly overview briefings" (events page) — resolve directly with the lab.
- **The most decision-relevant numbers are classified:** actual OPIR detection thresholds, fire-control track-accuracy requirements, and warn-to-decision latency budgets. Every quantitative claim in your proposal should be framed as relative/notional and sourced to public literature.
- **Timeline figures cited here are illustrative open-literature values** (e.g., ~30 s legacy detect-to-alert, ~180 s ICBM boost), not current requirements; present them as such.
- **Program details and cost figures move fast.** Constellation counts, contractor assignments, launch dates, and Golden Dome specifics are shifting month to month (e.g., the White House's $175B vs. CBO's $1.2T Golden Dome estimates reflect different scopes and time horizons); verify against the latest SDA/SSC/GAO releases and the live DSIP posting before submission, and confirm the exact DSIP deadline time (DoW CSO deadlines are typically 12:00 p.m. Eastern, not end of day).
- **Boulderlab.org gates detailed content behind registration**, and some pages resist automated access; confirm current cohort/briefing specifics, current OPIR TAP Lab leadership, and the SBIR-awardee onboarding workflow directly with the lab.
