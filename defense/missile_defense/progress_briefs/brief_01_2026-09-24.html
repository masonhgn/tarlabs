Progress brief 01

AI/ML for missile detection, warning and tracking — SBIR topic DAF26BX06-NV510
24 September 2026 · written for Mason · plain-language snapshot plus a self-audit

The one-paragraph version. We have built a careful, well-checked foundation: a simulator of satellites, targets, atmosphere and background, plus two classical trackers proven correct against outside references. We have not yet built the part that turns a target's position into what a satellite actually sees, and we have not written any AI. We decided the proposal is about a new AI/ML tracking contribution; the literature showed the idea we first had (learning which models a tracker runs) is already published three times, so the contribution must be sharper. Before any AI work, we need a fair, leak-proof test bench. The self-audit at the end found real shortcuts — most seriously, none of this work is saved in version control yet.

1. What we are trying to do

The Space Force wants to know whether AI/ML can help satellites that watch for missiles in infrared light (called OPIR) detect, track and warn about them better. Phase I is not a product; it is a feasibility study: a simulation, analysis and plan showing an idea is worth funding. It is judged on four things — accuracy, latency, resilience, coverage — and it must show the AI is explainable and says how confident it is.

Everything is unclassified. Real missile-warning satellite data is secret, so everything we show will come from simulation built on public information. The government expects this for Phase I.

What the foundation has already taught us

  • Speed of light is not the bottleneck. Signals travel from satellite to ground in hundredths of a second; warnings take tens of seconds. So "process on the satellite to be faster" only wins if the processing itself is faster there — not because the satellite is closer.
  • Comparing consecutive images lets you see about 13× fainter things than looking at one image, and faster revisits make targets visible that are otherwise hidden.
  • The "smarter" tracker is cheaper, not more accurate. A survey claimed it was much better. The original paper actually shows it is slightly less accurate and about half the cost. A second, independent paper agrees. We now trust primary sources over summaries.
  • Almost every latency number anyone produces is assumption (over 99.5%), so we should report comparisons and break-even conditions, not absolute times.

5. What is "learned model-set adaptation"?

The tracker as a committee. A manoeuvring target might fly straight, turn left hard, climb, or turn right gently. A classical tracker (the IMM) runs a committee of forecasters, each assuming one kind of motion. Every time a new observation arrives, each forecaster is scored on how well it predicted it; the committee's answer is a weighted blend, and the weights are themselves useful: "80% sure it is turning left."

Fixed versus variable committee. The basic version consults the whole committee every time — reliable but expensive. The "variable-structure" version only invites the members who seem relevant right now, plus the neighbours of whoever is currently winning. LMS is a specific rulebook for that: drop anyone below 0.01% belief; if anyone is above 30%, invite the manoeuvres next to theirs.

"Learned" model-set adaptation means replacing that hand-written rulebook with a trained AI policy that decides who gets invited each moment.

Where that idea stands. Three research groups have already published it (2023, 2024, 2025). On honest tests it gives a few percent. None of them checked whether the tracker's confidence stays trustworthy afterwards, none used satellite-style angle-only sensing, and none tested on motion patterns the AI had never seen. Those gaps are real, but the idea itself is not new.

Where the AI would go. Stage 4 is the natural home — tracking a target that manoeuvres, seen only as directions from moving satellites. Stage 3 (detection in images) is the alternative.

What must come first regardless. Stage 2 and a fair test bench ("Phase B"): many kinds of simulated flights, whole categories held back so the AI cannot memorise the simulator, the same random conditions for every method, error bars on every comparison, and the classical tracker tuned as hard as the AI. Without this, any AI result is unfalsifiable.

8. Self-audit: where we cut corners

You asked whether we have taken serious shortcuts. Yes, several. In order of seriousness:

  1. No version control. All 23 modules, 303 tests, the plan and the analysis exist only as files on disk — zero commits. One bad overwrite or disk problem loses everything, and we cannot show a reviewer the history. This is the single most serious gap and the cheapest to fix.
  2. Second-hand sources, three times. I built the first VS-IMM from a survey's description instead of the paper; recorded a "failed replication" of a claim I had not traced; and called learned model-set adaptation an "open problem" without searching. Each was caught, but the pattern is mine and it is recurring.
  3. Research reports largely unverified. The second deep-research report has about 90 citations, none of them resolvable as delivered; I checked 4. Its conclusions shaped our plan.
  4. "Done" that is thinner than its name. Target motion is marked done with one flight path. The data phase passed in roughly a day across 24 domains; "understood" was claimed for aircraft data we have never run through code.
  5. The replication has two loose ends we explained rather than investigated: our random scenario is 13% easier than the paper's, and the paper's accuracy gain for its "forgetting factor" variant only partly reproduced.
  6. Compliance and outreach untouched. The window to contact the program officers directly closed on 23 September. Registrations that gate submission have not started.
  7. Housekeeping drift. The project instructions still say we are in the data phase; the assumptions register has 26 rows for far more numbers than that; there is no one-command figure generator yet.
