## ARM26BX03-NP006 — xTech|Inversion Competition

**ARMY / 34** · triage verdict: **NOT IN SURVIVORS**

**Q1.** Can Reactor 2 operate independently using an externally supplied ethylene stream, or is the integrated two-reactor configuration required for practical operation?

**A1.** Yes, Reactor 2 can operate independently using an externally supplied ethylene stream.

**Q2.** Although methanol is listed among the potential oxygenated feedstocks in the patent, all experimental examples appear to be based on aqueous ethanol. Has the reactor architecture been experimentally evaluated using methanol feedstocks, or is methanol compatibility currently only conceptual?

**A2.** No, we did not try the methanol. We were focusing on ethanol study.

**Q3.** What Technology Readiness Level (TRL) range is the Army targeting for participating solutions?

**A3.** This competition is TRL agnostic.

**Q4.** Is using a selected IP set as a module within a broader, company-owned product an acceptable approach, or does the proposal need to treat the IP set as a standalone product?

**A4.** A company could choose to develop an ARL invention/patent as either a ‘module’ to add to or complement their own products/IP or a stand-alone product. Either way, companies will need to lay out/describe how they intend to use/commercialize the licensed government IP if and when they submit a formal patent license application.

**Q5.** For IP set 8, how should applicants address the operational sustainment model, including lifecycle cost, readiness impact, maintenance forecasting, deployment economics, and audit-ready accountability?

**A5.** Applicants should describe how the proposed solution supports long-term operation in field environments, including calibration stability, sensor reliability, installation and replacement requirements, and expected maintenance intervals. White papers should also address deployment and lifecycle costs, impact on operational readiness through reduced installation complexity or downtime, and approaches for data integrity, traceability, and auditability of power monitoring records. Quantitative estimates are encouraged where available.

**Q6.** For the Phase I Army SBIR, does the proof of concept need to focus only on the IP set itself, or can it include integrating the IP set into the applicant's host platform as the demonstration?

**A6.** Either approach is feasible. The company should do what makes the most sense for them.

**Q7.** What intellectual property rights will participating companies retain, and what rights does the Army expect?

**A7.** This depends on the company's intent and is best addressed through the patent license application process. Interested parties should reach out to the appropriate tech transfer office to discuss this further.

**Q8.** Can a small business acquire the patent license outside of the xTech Inversion program?

**A8.** Yes, you can apply for a license outside of this program. You should get in touch with the tech transfer office of the responsible Army laboratory.

**Q9.** How should applicants handle IP rights when combining Army-owned IP with their own developed software, data models, or algorithms?

**A9.** A licensing process would need to be followed. This will be covered in more detail during the finalist accelerator, but it involves submitting a patent license application.

**Q10.** For IP set 12, what about proposing an open architecture that can adapt to applications and specs that will be defined in the future?

**A10.** Open architecture approaches are fine and often desirable, but only if they remain grounded in real converter physics and commercialization pathways. They should still define controllable boundaries—like topology choice, regulation method, and expected performance ranges—so we can assess feasibility and transition potential, not just conceptual flexibility.

**Q11.** IP set 12, what is the physical form factor of the converter circuit?

**A11.** It was demonstrated on a rigid PCB, but other substrates are possible. The active part of the converter is very small, about 0.34 square inches (roughly the size of a dime).

**Q12.** IP set 12, what is the minimum input voltage the converter has been validated to step up from, and what is the output voltage range?

**A12.** The converter can self-start at less than 20 millivolts and provide an output of up to 2.5 volts. It has demonstrated a step-up from 35 millivolts input to a 2.5-volt output with over 400 microwatts of power at 58% efficiency. The desired output is typically regulated at 1.8 to 3.3 volts.

**Q13.** IP set 12, has this converter been tested in combination with IP Set 11 (Dual Coil Inductive Energy Generator)?

**A13.** No, they were developed separately and have not been tested together.

**Q14.** For IP set 12, how should the white paper address that the topics are very light on technical specs?

**A14.** The white papers should explicitly treat missing technical specs as part of the problem definition. Participants are expected to make reasonable engineering assumptions for low-voltage step-up converters and clearly state them—especially input range, power levels, efficiency targets, and intended application domains. The quality signal is how well they define and justify a viable commercialization envelope, not whether they guess an exact spec correctly.

**Q15.** Has the harvester been tested in any continuous low-G oscillation environment — vibration table, pendulum, or wrist-worn prototype — as opposed to single-event setback testing?

**A15.** Setback generators designed for fusing, need to keep an explosive round and it’s connected circuitry de-energized until it sees gun-launch. The shear disc tightly confines the magnetic and it is help in the upper position until the gun-launch event shears the disk, allowing the magnet to displace. Small reserve batteries can deliver around 100 mW of power and the M203 flight duration is around 30 sec. When setback generators approach this amount of energy interesting new applications are realized. The maximum magnet velocity is around 10 m/s, thus a setback generator for an M203 maximum energy produced approaches 350 mJ of kinetic energy.

**Q16.** What materials comprise the cylindrical enclosure, magnet, and coils — specifically whether any ferromagnetic materials are present that would interact with a brass or aluminum housing?

**A16.** Cartech SS430F, Neodymium D42B-N52, coil was plastic, no brass or aluminum.

**Q17.** IP set 12, the topic is light on technical specs. How should the white paper address this? Should we define the specs ourselves?

**A17.** The patent was written broadly. The technology was originally developed for wearable thermoelectric generators on skin. White papers should treat missing specs as part of the problem, state justified engineering assumptions, and define clear performance and physical boundaries so feasibility can be assessed. Open architecture approaches are acceptable.

**Q18.** For IP set 11, what is the output voltage and current profile of the generator?

**A18.** For the specified requirements, it charged a 120-microfarad capacitor to 13.7 volts. The power curve mimicked the gun-launch acceleration profile, and the coil winding resistance was approximately 0.7 ohms.

**Q19.** For IP set 11, what was the measured power output in laboratory testing?

**A19.** For the M203 platform, the generator produced 9 millijoules of energy, charging a 120-microfarad capacitor to 13.7 volts. For the higher velocity Mark 19 platform, it is estimated that 30 to 60 millijoules could be generated.

**Q20.** For IP set 11, what were the original design G-force parameters, and has any variant been tested at a lower acceleration threshold (e.g., 8-50G range)?

**A20.** The M203 and the Mk19 setback acceleration profiles were tested. Two different shear disc designs were used for the respective gun-launch accelerations. Nothing lower than 11,000 g’s with a 1.25 ms pulse duration has been tested. The magnet, in the non-displaced position, is tightly held in the upper magnetic loop where the magnetic flux is confined. So 50 g’s might not be enough static acceleration to break the pull force of around 4 lbs. This equates to roughly 1200 g’s.

**Q21.** For IP set 4, Would the Army view predictive maintenance, cost/readiness analysis, and sustainment reporting as valuable commercialization extensions of the sensor technology?

**A21.** As a research lab, the Chemical Biological Center (CBC) would not perform these analyses at the R&D level. This would typically occur later when a technology transitions to a Program of Record. However, a patent licensing partner may choose to conduct this type of analysis as part of their business development.

**Q22.** Can the Army provide samples of the zirconium material or fibers during the Phase I feasibility effort?

**A22.** If a sufficient sample amount exists, providing it under a commercial evaluation license could be discussed and possibly included in the license terms. Another option is a CRADA, which might require cost reimbursement, to fabricate the material.

**Q23.** Will awardees have access to the underlying fabrication methods and technical data associated with the zirconium hydroxide technology?

**A23.** A commercial evaluation license would cover data directly related to the patent. Discussing separate, underlying fabrication methods and data could be possible under a Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA), provided there is sufficient benefit to the Army.

**Q24.** Can the zirconium hydroxide material be used as a coating on a chemical sensor for detection, rather than being incorporated into textiles?

**A24.** Using it as a sensor coating can certainly be considered and discussed. It has not been a topic the Army has pursued in depth, but a commercial evaluation license would allow for a more detailed discussion.

**Q25.** For software-focused small businesses, is the Army open to proposals that commercialize one of the Army IP sets by building a complementary software, analytics, governance, sustainment, or commercialization layer around the IP, rather than manufacturing the physical device or material itself?

**A25.** This is your opportunity to showcase your solution. If your solution is focused on one of the IP sets within the RFI, then we encourage you to submit a proposal for consideration. We’re looking forward to seeing a range of creative and impactful solutions and discovering how your expertise can contribute to advancing Army initiatives.

**Q26.** Does the xTech Inversion competition allow for combining IP sets in one proposal?

**A26.** No, only one IP set should be submitted for each proposal, as outlined in the RFI.

**Q27.** What is the preferred way to disclose the pending proposal to the Program Office, as Section II directs?

**A27.** Email the disclosure to the xTech mailbox.

**Q28.** Does the Army prefer white papers that focus on direct Army transition first, commercial market adoption first, or a dual-use path?

**A28.** Please refer to the evaluation criteria listed on the xTech|Inversion Valid Eval signup page for guidance on this.

**Q29.** What characteristics distinguish the most successful xTech submissions in previous competitions?

**A29.** Proposals should provide structured, detailed explanations of their proposed solutions while incorporating supporting data, relevant research, and evidence of prior testing or proven performance to establish technical credibility. Additionally, Clearly and directly address the specified evaluation criteria, enabling reviewers to efficiently verify how the proposed solution meets each dimension.

**Q30.** What level of sponsorship or transition support is provided to winning technologies to bridge the gap after a successful demonstration?

**A30.** The program offers an accelerator for finalists, with additional programming for winners. Furthermore, individuals selected for an SBIR award will be paired with a technical point of contact (who may be the subject matter expert or inventor) for the duration of the contract.

**Q31.** Does the Army anticipate opportunities for dual-use commercial technologies that can be adapted for military applications?

**A31.** The xTech Program is always looking for dual-use technologies. For this competition, proposals should focus on developing one of the internal Army IP sets listed in the Request for Information (RFI) and its dual-use potential.

**Q32.** Is there a pathway for xTech participation into Army Programs of Record, Other Transaction Authority (OTA) contracts, or operational pilot programs?

**A32.** The transition pathway depends on the specific xTech competition. For this competition, winners will be eligible to submit a Phase I SBIR proposal worth up to $300,000.

**Q33.** Are prototype demonstrations expected in laboratory environments, operational environments, or field exercises?

**A33.** Demonstrations are not part of this specific competition. They may, however, be part of follow-on activities for companies that are awarded a Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) contract. The context for any demonstration would be determined at that time.

**Q34.** In inversion, is the attempt to reverse operational assumptions, counter adversary system architectures, or restructure decision and data representation layers?

**A34.** Reversing operational assumptions: Yes, this competition is a strategic shift. Instead of the traditional "pull" for custom designs from industry, the Army is "pushing" its existing intellectual property (IP) out to industry for them to develop commercialization plans. Countering adversary systems: This is considered a secondary benefit. The primary goal is to increase technology transition from Army labs and build relationships with non-traditional partners. Restructuring data layers: This is also a secondary benefit to the overall goal of shifting from a "pull" to a "push" model for technology transfer.
